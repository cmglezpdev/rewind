# Attachment to tools and learning unfamiliar ways to work

## Why technical preferences persist

- Theo argues that engineers often become attached to the layer that was important when they entered the field. He compares this to keeping the musical preferences formed during adolescence. [`23:58`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1438) [`24:26`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1466)
- His own attachment was to framework debates, including Next.js, SvelteKit, Solid, and Nuxt. He describes later moving toward infrastructure and then agents as the industry conversation changed. [`24:26`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1466) [`25:20`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1520) [`25:59`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1559)
- He recounts meeting Boris, the creator of Claude Code, who knew him as an AI developer without knowing his web-development history. Theo uses this anecdote to illustrate how quickly professional identities can shift. [`26:26`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1586) [`26:40`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1600) [`27:05`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1625) [`27:32`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1652)

## What the skateboarding analogy teaches

- "Switch" means performing a trick in the opposite stance from the skater's usual one. In skateboarding, learning both stances is valued even when outsiders cannot see the extra difficulty. [`30:57`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1857) [`31:28`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1888) [`31:59`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1919)
- He compares unfamiliar stances to writing Go after years of JavaScript. Existing habits may carry over even when the new environment calls for different patterns. [`32:23`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=1943)
- Mastery and adaptability can pull in different directions. Theo says specialization can be lucrative, but becoming deeply comfortable with one technique can make changing harder. These are personal observations, not career outcome data. [`33:32`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2012) [`33:55`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2035) [`34:46`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2086)
- Learning switch tricks taught him to enjoy starting again despite feeling unskilled. He applies that attitude to moving between frameworks, languages, and agent-based work. [`34:22`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2062) [`35:24`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2124)

## Opinions, predictions, and a counterexample

- Theo uses resistance to TypeScript as an example of sunk effort influencing tool preferences. This is his interpretation of other developers' behavior. [`35:51`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2151) [`36:17`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2177) [`36:50`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2210)
- His prediction that many committed Vim users will lose software jobs over the next five years is a provocative forecast, not an established result. He also explicitly praises Vim and Neovim. [`37:16`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2236) [`37:36`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2256)
- He extends the warning to loyalty toward a model family. A currently strong family can lose its advantage, so he argues against refusing alternatives. [`38:23`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ebILGCoN4UU&t=2303)
- The Effect creator, Michael, challenged Theo's assumption that deep TypeScript expertise implied resistance to moving on. Michael's post said that using T3 Code had made him stop caring which harness was underneath.
