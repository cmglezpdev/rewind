# Fixing the source of bad contributions

## The misleading documentation example

1. Theo submitted a poor PR after following the rewrite documentation and existing code examples. A colleague, Derek, asked why he had chosen that implementation. [`12:38`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=758)
2. Theo showed the documentation and examples that had led him there. Within about 30 minutes, Derek opened two PRs: one corrected the misleading documentation, and one rewrote the confusing examples. [`13:01`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=781)
3. The lesson was to improve the material that generates mistakes. A bad contribution can reveal a repeatable failure in the environment, and leaving it unresolved lets quality erode. [`13:30`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=810) [`13:55`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=835)

## Quality as an organizational problem

- Theo argues that code quality depends on communication and organization as well as individual engineering skill. The goal is for both an average and an excellent engineer to produce contributions that are safe to merge. [`15:53`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=953)
- This part of his argument centers on the feedback relationship between product and platform. He explicitly distinguishes what he has described from management, intensive code review, coverage targets, and extensive test rules. [`16:24`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=984) [`16:44`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=1004)
- His operating goal at Twitch was to make the correct approach easy and remove examples of the incorrect approach. Faster delivery was a consequence, rather than the sole objective. [`17:25`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtJKFhztgdM&t=1045)
