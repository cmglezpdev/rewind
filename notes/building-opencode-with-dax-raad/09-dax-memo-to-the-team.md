# 09 — Dax's Memo: Three Problems Turbocharged by LLMs

Dax sent an internal memo naming three challenges — none new, but all "turbocharged by LLMs" [`48:47`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=2927):

1. Shipping features not worth shipping.
2. When iterating, the original design turns out off, forcing something hacky — and the LLM absorbs the hack invisibly.
3. Needing to spend more time cleaning things up.

## 1. Features not worth shipping

- It's too easy to respond to any problem by shipping a feature. The fix is restraint: explicitly defining what kind of thing they ship now versus what waits for more clarity [`49:19`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=2959).

## 2. The muted prickle — hacks without feedback

- The classic dilemma: the system doesn't support the new feature, so you either redesign from first principles or absorb a temporary hack. That's a judgment call — and "that judgment is so distorted right now because the agent will just do the hacky thing for you" [`49:40`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=2980), [`50:09`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3009).
- Result: OpenCode shipped "way more hacks in places where we should have rethought the whole system" [`50:34`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3034).
- The mechanism: pre-AI, writing a hack came with a bad feeling — a "prickle" — that accumulated with each subsequent hack and kept judgment sharp. Agents mute it: someone else deals with the problem, the landmines still blow up later, but you don't feel it, so the feedback loop is gone [`50:56`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3056), [`51:37`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3097).
- Analogy: the CEO who delegates everything and never sees the shop floor versus hands-on leaders (e.g., Stripe's CTO periodically doing support/coding rotations). "All of life is about having the proper feedback loops in place" [`51:57`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3117), [`52:24`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3144).

## 3. Cleaning things up

- The daily pressure is real: thousands yelling for features, new competitors appearing constantly — "if we let ourselves get pulled by those forces, I don't think it results in anything good" [`52:46`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3166).
- The silver lining: **cleanup is easier than ever with agents.** Pre-AI, a new pattern only applied going forward; now you can ask the agent to implement it everywhere, kill dead patterns, and pay down tech debt cheaply [`53:10`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3190), [`53:40`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3220).
- Why bother when it brings no revenue: "there's a million ways to be a successful company… I want to make sure we build a place that we're happy to work at 5 years from now" — not one that can't hire good people because the job is slogging through legacy [`54:01`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3241).

## The kicker

- "The worst part about all of this is I don't think we're trading this off to move faster. I think we're moving at a normal pace" — it *feels* fast, but they're not outpacing competitors, and it's easy to trick yourself about productivity [`54:39`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3279), [`55:01`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3301).
- His counter-philosophy: prepare a lot, set the foundations, "and then when you spring, you spring with so much more force" [`55:22`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1VqKUrxR2C8&t=3322).
